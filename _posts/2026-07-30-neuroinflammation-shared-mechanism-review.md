**Abstract.** Neuroinflammation has emerged as a candidate shared mechanism linking Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), and multiple sclerosis (MS), three neurological disorders with distinct clinical presentations but overlapping innate immune features. This narrative review synthesizes published meta-analyses, systematic reviews, PET studies, and postmortem investigations examining TNF-α, IL-6, and microglial activation markers across the three diseases, with the goal of determining whether the evidence supports a unified inflammatory model or points instead toward disease-specific mechanisms that converge only at the level of downstream effector molecules. Across all three conditions, peripheral and central elevations of TNF-α and IL-6 are consistently reported, and TSPO-PET imaging demonstrates increased microglial activation relative to healthy controls, converging on a shared toll-like receptor / NF-κB signaling architecture. However, the anatomical distribution, temporal trajectory, and cellular compartmentalization of this activation differ substantially across the three diseases, and randomized controlled trials of anti-inflammatory agents have produced markedly different outcomes, with immune-modulating therapies transforming the clinical course of MS while comparable approaches have shown limited efficacy in AD and PD.

## The question

AD, PD, and MS look nothing alike clinically, but all three involve the same basic immune signature: activated microglia and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, chiefly TNF-α and IL-6. That overlap raises an obvious question — is this one shared disease mechanism wearing three different clinical masks, something a single class of anti-inflammatory drug could in principle treat across all three? Or is it a shared downstream *effect* that different diseases arrive at through entirely different routes? This review works through the biomarker literature, the imaging literature, and — most decisively — the clinical trial record to answer that.

## Where the evidence converges

The molecular machinery really is shared. Microglia detect pathological signals — amyloid-beta and tau in AD, aggregated alpha-synuclein in PD, myelin-derived antigens in MS — through toll-like receptors that funnel into the same NF-κB signaling cascade, producing the same downstream cytokines regardless of what triggered them. That's borne out empirically: meta-analyses report significantly elevated peripheral TNF-α and IL-6 in AD (40 studies), in PD (13 studies, 898 patients), and in MS. In vivo TSPO-PET imaging, which visualizes activated microglia directly, shows elevated signal across all three conditions relative to healthy controls — a transdiagnostic meta-analysis of 156 case-control studies confirmed this pattern, with AD showing among the largest cortical elevations of any category studied.

## Where it breaks apart

That's where the similarity ends. The *where* and *why* of microglial activation differ in ways that matter:

- **Anatomy.** AD activation concentrates in cortico-limbic and temporo-parietal regions, tracking tau spread. PD activation is comparatively restricted to the midbrain. MS activation is compartmentalized into focal white-matter lesions, the iron-rich rims of chronic "smoldering" lesions, and normal-appearing white matter — a fundamentally different spatial pattern from either.
- **Trigger.** In AD and PD, microglia are reacting to misfolded proteins accumulating in the brain — an innate immune response to an internal danger signal. In MS, the initiating event is autoimmune: T and B lymphocytes mount an adaptive immune attack on myelin, with microglia acting as downstream amplifiers rather than first responders. That's a difference in kind, not just degree.
- **Cytokine signature.** A head-to-head comparison of CSF cytokines across AD, PD, and ALS found each disease had a distinguishable profile — AD showed elevated TGF-β, MCP-1, and YKL-40; PD showed elevated TGF-β1, IL-6, and IL-1β — rather than one interchangeable inflammatory fingerprint.
- **Peripheral vs. central signal.** In MS specifically, blood cytokine levels don't reliably track disease activity in the brain, meaning peripheral panels can't be treated as a clean proxy for CNS inflammation in any of these diseases.

## The evidence that actually settles it: clinical trials

Biomarkers can only say so much about causation. The clearer signal comes from what happens when you intervene. In MS, immune-modulating therapies — interferons, glatiramer acetate, natalizumab, anti-CD20 antibodies — have transformed the disease course, substantially cutting relapse rates. In AD, the one placebo-controlled trial of a TNF-α inhibitor (etanercept, n=41) showed no significant benefit on cognition or function. In PD, minocycline has similarly failed to show clear neuroprotection in early trials. If inflammation played the same causal role in all three diseases, this asymmetry would be hard to explain. It's much easier to explain under a model where immune activation is the primary driver in MS, but a downstream amplifier in AD and PD.

## Bottom line

The evidence doesn't support treating neuroinflammation as one unified disease mechanism showing up in three places — the triggers, anatomy, cytokine signatures, and treatment responses are too different for that. But it also doesn't support writing off the overlap as coincidence. The most defensible read is an intermediate model: a shared downstream inflammatory effector system, built on conserved microglial machinery, recruited by fundamentally different upstream processes — causally primary in MS, amplifying and secondary in AD and PD. Practically, that means future meta-analyses pooling these diseases together need to treat disease identity as a real moderator, not noise to average over.
